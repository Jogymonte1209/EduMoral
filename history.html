Religious History

The Story of Faith Through the Ages

Follow the Bible’s unfolding story from creation and the first covenant to Jesus, the early church, and the faith communities that carried the gospel across generations. Each chapter includes passages to read alongside the historical overview.

Creation Patriarchs Exodus Judges Kingdom Exile & return Between the Testaments Jesus Early church Faith through the ages

1. Creation and the Human Story

Genesis 1–11

“In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth.” The Bible opens with a world brought into being by God, described as good and full of life. Humanity is made in God’s image and entrusted with caring for creation. These opening chapters establish dignity, responsibility, and relationship as key themes of the story.

Genesis also describes the fracture caused by human disobedience, followed by violence and the flood. Yet judgment is accompanied by mercy: God preserves life and makes a covenant with Noah. The story introduces a recurring pattern—human failure, divine faithfulness, and the possibility of a new beginning.

2. The Patriarchs and God’s Promise

Genesis 12–50

God calls Abraham to leave his home and journey toward an unknown land, promising to make him a blessing to all families of the earth. The promise continues through Isaac and Jacob, whose family becomes the people of Israel. Their lives include conflict, uncertainty, and moments of trust, showing that the covenant story unfolds through imperfect people.

Joseph, one of Jacob’s sons, is taken to Egypt and eventually rises to leadership there. During a famine, his family joins him, setting the stage for the Israelites’ later hardship and deliverance from Egypt.

3. Exodus, Covenant, and the Wilderness

Exodus–Deuteronomy

In Exodus, the Israelites are enslaved in Egypt. God calls Moses to lead them out, and the escape from slavery becomes a defining memory of deliverance. At Mount Sinai, the people receive the covenant and the Law, which shapes their worship and calls them to live with justice, faithfulness, and care for one another.

The wilderness years test the community’s trust. The books of Leviticus, Numbers, and Deuteronomy preserve laws, journeys, and Moses’ teaching before the people enter the land. The Exodus remains central to Israel’s identity and is remembered throughout the Bible as a sign of God’s power to save.

4. The Land, the Judges, and a People in Transition

Joshua–Ruth

The book of Joshua tells of Israel’s entry into Canaan and the division of the land among the tribes. In the period that follows, the tribes are not ruled by a single king. Judges such as Deborah, Gideon, and Samson lead during times of danger and instability, while the book of Ruth offers a quieter story of loyalty and belonging within this era.

The biblical account describes a repeated cycle: the people turn away, face hardship, call for help, and are delivered. These stories set up the desire for stable leadership and raise questions about what faithful leadership should look like.

5. The United and Divided Kingdoms

1 Samuel–2 Chronicles

Israel’s monarchy begins with Saul, followed by David, who establishes Jerusalem as the political center. David’s son Solomon builds the Temple, a central place of worship. The biblical narrative links the royal line with a promise of lasting significance, while also showing how power and personal failure can bring harm.

After Solomon, the kingdom divides into Israel in the north and Judah in the south. Prophets—including Elijah, Isaiah, and Jeremiah—call rulers and people back to worship, justice, and care for the vulnerable. Their warnings speak to the consequences of injustice and idolatry, while their promises hold out hope for renewal.

6. Exile, Return, and Rebuilding

2 Kings 17–25; Ezra–Nehemiah

The biblical books of Kings describe the fall of the northern kingdom and, later, the destruction of Jerusalem and the exile of many people of Judah to Babylon. Exile brought loss of land, Temple, and political independence. In this difficult setting, the prophets continued to speak of God’s presence and a future restoration.

Under Persian rule, some exiles returned to Jerusalem. They rebuilt the Temple and, later, the city walls. The books of Ezra and Nehemiah describe efforts to renew communal life around Scripture and worship. The return brought hope, but the people still awaited the fuller restoration described by the prophets.

7. Between the Testaments

A bridge to the New Testament

In the centuries before the New Testament period, Jewish communities lived under a succession of empires and political powers. Greek language and culture spread widely, and later Roman rule shaped the world in which the New Testament events took place. Jewish life remained diverse, with different communities and movements interpreting Scripture and practicing their faith in distinct ways.

This period helps explain the setting of the Gospels: synagogues, pilgrimage to Jerusalem, Roman officials, and widespread expectation for God to act. It is a historical bridge rather than a silent gap between the two parts of the Bible.

8. Jesus Christ: The Center of the Christian Story

Matthew–John

The Gospels describe Jesus’ birth, ministry, teaching, and works of healing in the Roman-ruled land of Judea and Galilee. He announces the kingdom of God, welcomes people on the margins, challenges hypocrisy, and teaches love of God and neighbor. His disciples come to recognize him as the promised Messiah.

Jesus is crucified under Roman authority. The Gospels then proclaim his resurrection, which Christians understand as God’s victory over sin and death. His life, death, and resurrection form the foundation of Christian faith and the turning point around which the New Testament story is told.

9. The Early Church and the Gospel’s Expansion

Acts; Romans–Revelation

Acts begins with Jesus’ followers in Jerusalem and describes the growth of the church after Pentecost. The message travels through Judea and Samaria and into cities across the Roman world. Paul and other missionaries establish and encourage communities, bringing the gospel to both Jewish and non-Jewish audiences.

Letters in the New Testament address the beliefs, questions, and practical challenges of these communities. They teach about grace, unity, worship, and daily life. Revelation offers hope to communities facing pressure, presenting a vision of God’s justice and the renewal of creation.

10. Faith Through the Ages

A continuing story

After the New Testament period, Christian communities continued to grow and take root in different cultures. Across history, believers have translated Scripture, formed communities of worship, served neighbors, and debated how best to live out their faith. Their story includes both acts of compassion and chapters of failure, which invite honest reflection rather than a simplified account.

Studying this history can connect big events with personal faith: the call to trust, the work of justice and mercy, and the hope of restoration. Readers can trace these themes in the Bible and consider how they shape faithful choices today.

How to keep exploring: Read the Scripture references for each chapter, notice how covenant and hope develop over time, and compare how each biblical book contributes to the larger story.
